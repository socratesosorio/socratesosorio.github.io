Part 0: Camera Calibration and Pose Estimation

Overview

Before we can train a neural radiance field, we need to know where our cameras are positioned in 3D space. This part implements camera calibration using ArUco markers and pose estimation using OpenCV's solvePnP algorithm.

Camera Calibration

Camera calibration determines the intrinsic parameters of the camera (focal length, principal point, distortion coefficients). I used ArUco markers (4x4 dictionary) placed on calibration images to find correspondences between 3D world coordinates and 2D image coordinates. OpenCV's cv2.calibrateCamera() then solves for the camera matrix K and distortion coefficients.

The calibration process detects ArUco tag corners in each image, matches them to known 3D world coordinates (corners at (0,0,0), (size,0,0), (size,size,0), (0,size,0)), and minimizes the reprojection error across all observations.

Pose Estimation

Once the camera is calibrated, I estimated the pose (rotation and translation) for each image containing the object of interest. Using cv2.solvePnP(), I found the world-to-camera transformation that best aligns the detected ArUco corners with their known 3D positions. This gives us the rotation vector (rvec) and translation vector (tvec).

The world-to-camera transformation is then inverted to obtain the camera-to-world transformation (c2w), which describes where the camera is positioned in the world coordinate system. This is what NeRF needs for training.

Dataset Creation

After pose estimation, I undistorted all images using cv2.undistort() to remove lens distortion. The images were cropped to the valid region of interest (ROI) determined by cv2.getOptimalNewCameraMatrix(). Finally, the dataset was split into train (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets and saved as an npz file.

Part 1: Neural Field for 2D Image Representation

Overview

A neural field is a continuous function represented by a neural network. For 2D images, the neural field maps normalized pixel coordinates $(u, v) \in [0,1]^2$ to RGB colors $(r, g, b) \in [0,1]^3$ The network learns to fit the image by minimizing the mean squared error between predicted and ground truth pixel colors.

Positional Encoding

Neural networks are biased toward learning low-frequency functions. To enable them to represent high-frequency details in images, we apply positional encoding to the input coordinates. The encoding transforms each coordinate x into:

$$\text{PE}(x) = [x, \sin(\pi x), \cos(\pi x), \sin(2\pi x), \cos(2\pi x), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi x), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi x)]$$

This encoding increases the input dimensionality from 2 to $2 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot L = 2 + 4L$. For $L=10$, the encoded input has 42 dimensions.

Network Architecture

The neural field is implemented as a simple multilayer perceptron (MLP) with 3 hidden layers, ReLU activations, and a sigmoid output layer to ensure colors are in [0,1]. The architecture is:

input $(42D)$ $\rightarrow$ Linear(width) $\rightarrow$ ReLU $\rightarrow$ Linear(width) $\rightarrow$ ReLU $\rightarrow$ Linear(width) $\rightarrow$ ReLU $\rightarrow$ Linear(3) $\rightarrow$ Sigmoid $\rightarrow$ RGB output

Implementation Details

  • Number of hidden layers: 3
  • Hidden layer width: 256 neurons per layer
  • Input dimension: 42 (2D coordinates + positional encoding with $L=10$)
  • Output dimension: 3 (RGB colors)
  • Activation functions: ReLU for hidden layers, Sigmoid for output
  • Positional encoding frequency levels: $L=10$
  • Learning rate: $1 \times 10^{-2}$
  • Optimizer: Adam
  • Loss function: Mean Squared Error (MSE)
  • Training iterations: 2000
  • Batch size: 10,000 random pixels per iteration

Training

The network is trained using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 1e-2. At each iteration, a batch of 10,000 random pixels is sampled from the image, and the network predicts their colors. The loss is the mean squared error between predictions and ground truth. Training runs for 2000 iterations, with PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio) computed to track reconstruction quality.

Hyperparameter Experiments

I tested different combinations of network width and positional encoding frequency to understand their effects on reconstruction quality.

Experiment Setup

  • width: 64 vs 256 neurons per hidden layer
  • max_freq_log2 (L): 3 vs 10

Lower frequency encoding (L=3) produces smoother, more blurry reconstructions because the network cannot represent high-frequency details. higher frequency encoding (L=10) enables sharp edges and fine textures. smaller network width (64) may underfit and produce worse reconstructions, while larger width (256) has more capacity to fit the image.

Part 2: Neural Radiance Field for 3D Scene Representation

Overview

Neural radiance fields (NeRF) extend the 2D neural field concept to 3D scenes. instead of mapping 2D coordinates to colors, NeRF maps 3D positions $(x, y, z)$ and viewing directions (normalized 3D vectors) to volume density $\sigma$ and RGB color $(r, g, b)$. by rendering multiple views through volume integration, the network learns a continuous 3D representation of the scene.

Ray Generation

For each pixel in a training image, I generate a ray that starts at the camera center and passes through the pixel. The ray is defined by its origin o and direction d. Points along the ray are parameterized as r(t) = o + td, where t  [near, far].

The implementation involves three coordinate transformations:

  1. pixel_to_camera: Convert pixel coordinates to normalized camera coordinates using the camera intrinsics
  2. transform_camera_to_world: Transform camera coordinates to world coordinates using the camera-to-world matrix (c2w)
  3. pixel_to_ray: Combine the above to generate ray origins and directions

Positional Encoding for 3D

Similar to part 1, positional encoding is applied to enable high-frequency details. For NeRF:

  • position encoding: $L=10$ frequency levels applied to $(x, y, z)$ $\rightarrow$ $3 + 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 10 = 63$ dimensions
  • direction encoding: $L=4$ frequency levels applied to $(x, y, z)$ (normalized direction vector) $\rightarrow$ $3 + 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 27$ dimensions

Network Architecture

The NeRF network is an 8-layer MLP with a skip connection at layer 5. The architecture is as follows (which is the same as the one in the project details!):

NeRF MLP network architecture diagram

This architecture allows the density to depend only on position (since density is view-independent), while color depends on both position and viewing direction (to model view-dependent effects like specular highlights).

Volume Rendering

To render a pixel, i sample N points along the ray and query the network at each point to get density σ and color c. the final pixel color is computed using the discrete volume rendering equation:

$$C = \sum_i T_i \cdot \alpha_i \cdot c_i \quad \text{where} \quad \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \cdot \delta_i), \quad T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad \delta_i \text{ is the step size}$$

Transmittance represents how much light reaches point i without being absorbed, and alpha represents how much light is absorbed at point i.

Training

Training uses the Adam optimizer with learning rate 5e-4. At each iteration, 10,000 random rays are sampled from the training images. For each ray, 64 points are sampled uniformly between near and far planes. The network predicts densities and colors, which are volume-rendered to produce pixel colors. The loss is the mean squared error between rendered and ground truth pixel colors.

Conclusion

This project demonstrated the power of neural implicit representations for both 2D and 3D vision tasks. In part 1, a simple MLP with positional encoding learned to represent images as continuous functions, achieving high-quality reconstructions. In part 2, NeRF extended this concept to 3D by combining neural networks with volume rendering, enabling photorealistic novel view synthesis from a set of calibrated 2D images. The custom scene results show that the entire pipeline (calibration, pose estimation, NeRF training) works end-to-end on real captured data.

Key Takeaways

  • Positional encoding is crucial for neural networks to represent high-frequency details
  • Neural fields provide a continuous, memory-efficient representation of visual data
  • NeRF's volume rendering formulation enables learning 3D structure from 2D observations
  • Accurate camera calibration and pose estimation are essential for training NeRF on real data
