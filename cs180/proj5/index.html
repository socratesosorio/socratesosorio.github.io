Part 1: Sampling Loops

1.1 Implementing the Forward Process

The forward process adds noise to a clean image according to the formula:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \epsilon \quad \text{where}~ \epsilon \sim N(0, 1)$$

This takes a clean image $x_0$ and produces a noisy version $x_t$ at timestep $t$. The noise level is controlled by $\bar\alpha_t$ from the noise schedule, where larger $t$ means more noise.

Noisy Campanile at Different Timesteps

Below shows the Campanile test image with noise added at timesteps 250, 500, and 750:

Campanile at t=250, t=500, and t=750 (progressively more noisy)

As expected, the image becomes progressively noisier as $t$ increases. At $t=250$, the structure is still clearly visible. At $t=500$, details are obscured but the general shape remains. At $t=750$, the image is nearly pure noise with only hints of the original structure.

1.2 Classical Denoising

Before using the diffusion model, I attempted to denoise the noisy images using classical methods - specifically Gaussian blur filtering. This approach attempts to smooth out the noise but cannot recover the original image details.

Gaussian blur denoising at different noise levels

The results show that Gaussian blur is ineffective at denoising these images. The blur removes some noise but also destroys image structure, resulting in blurry, indistinct outputs. This demonstrates why learned denoisers like diffusion models are necessary for high-quality image restoration.

1.3 One-Step Denoising

Using the pretrained DeepFloyd UNet denoiser, I performed one-step denoising on the noisy images. The UNet predicts the noise $\epsilon$ in the image, which can then be removed to estimate the clean image $x_0$.

The diffusion model UNet does a much better job than Gaussian blur. At lower noise levels (t=250), the reconstruction is quite good. However, as the noise level increases, the one-step denoising quality degrades significantly because the problem becomes much harder - there's simply too much noise to remove in a single step.

1.4 Iterative Denoising

Diffusion models are designed to denoise iteratively. Instead of removing all noise at once, we gradually remove noise over multiple steps using a strided timestep schedule. I used timesteps starting at 990, with stride 30, down to 0.

1.6 Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

Classifier-Free Guidance dramatically improves image quality by computing both conditional and unconditional noise estimates and combining them:

$$\epsilon = \epsilon_u + \gamma (\epsilon_c - \epsilon_u)$$

where $\gamma > 1$ amplifies the conditional signal. I used $\gamma = 7$ for these experiments.

5 sampled images with CFG ($\gamma = 7$)

The CFG samples are significantly better than the non-CFG samples from section 1.5. The images are more coherent, have better composition, and more closely match what one would expect from "a high quality photo" prompt.

1.7.2 Inpainting

Inpainting fills in masked regions of an image while preserving unmasked areas. At each denoising step, I replaced pixels outside the mask with the appropriately noised version of the original image:

$$x_t \leftarrow \mathbf{m} \cdot x_t + (1 - \mathbf{m}) \cdot \text{forward}(x_{orig}, t)$$

1.8 Visual Anagrams

Visual anagrams are optical illusions where an image looks like one thing, but when flipped upside down, reveals something else. This is achieved by averaging noise estimates from two different prompts - one for the upright image and one for the flipped version:

$$\epsilon_1 = \text{UNet}(x_t, t, p_1)$$ $$\epsilon_2 = \text{flip}(\text{UNet}(\text{flip}(x_t), t, p_2))$$ $$\epsilon = (\epsilon_1 + \epsilon_2) / 2$$

Part B: Flow Matching from Scratch

Overview

In Part B, I trained my own flow matching model on the MNIST dataset from scratch. Flow matching is a generative modeling technique that learns to transform samples from a simple distribution (like Gaussian noise) into samples from a complex data distribution by learning the "flow" or velocity field between them.

The key idea is to define a linear interpolation path between noise $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and clean data $x_1$:

$$x_t = (1-t)x_0 + tx_1 \quad \text{where } x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1), t \in [0, 1] \tag{B.3}$$

The flow (velocity) along this path is the derivative with respect to time:

$$u(x_t, t) = \frac{d}{dt} x_t = x_1 - x_0 \tag{B.4}$$

Our UNet $u_\theta(x_t, t)$ learns to predict this flow.

Part 1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

1.2 Noising Process Visualization

Before training the denoiser, I visualized the noising process at different noise levels $\sigma$. The noising process adds Gaussian noise to a clean image:

$$z = x + \sigma \epsilon, \quad \text{where } \epsilon \sim N(0, I) \tag{B.2}$$

Below shows an MNIST digit with increasing noise levels from $\sigma = 0.0$ (clean) to $\sigma = 1.0$ (very noisy):

Noising process at $\sigma = [0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0]$

As expected, the image becomes progressively harder to recognize as $\sigma$ increases. At $\sigma = 0.5$ (our training noise level), the digit structure is still somewhat visible but significantly degraded. At $\sigma = 1.0$, the image is almost pure noise.

1.2.1 Training the Denoiser

The goal is to train a denoiser $D_\theta$ that maps noisy image $z$ to clean image $x$. We optimize over an L2 loss:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_{z,x} \|D_{\theta}(z) - x\|^2 \tag{B.1}$$

I trained an unconditional UNet to denoise images with noise level $\sigma = 0.5$. The training setup was:

  • Dataset: MNIST training set
  • Batch size: 256
  • Learning rate: 1e-4
  • Hidden dimension: D = 128
  • Epochs: 5
  • Loss: MSE (L2) loss between predicted and original clean image

The denoiser was trained specifically on $\sigma = 0.5$. To test its generalization, I applied it to images with different noise levels $\sigma = [0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0]$:

The model performs best at its training noise level ($\sigma = 0.5$). For lower noise levels ($\sigma < 0.5$), the model tends to over-denoise, sometimes altering the digit structure. This demonstrates the importance of matching training and inference conditions.

Discussion

Observation: The outputs tend to look like a blurry average of all MNIST digits, rather than distinct, recognizable digits.

Explanation: This is because with MSE loss, the model learns to predict the point that minimizes the sum of squared distances to all training examples. Since pure noise has no correlation with any specific digit, the optimal prediction under MSE is the mean/centroid of all training images - essentially an average of all digits 0-9. This results in a blurry, indistinct output that looks like overlapping digits.

This demonstrates why single-step denoising fails for generative tasks and motivates the need for iterative denoising approaches like flow matching.

Part 2: Training a Flow Matching Model

Overview

Since single-step denoising doesn't work for generation, we turn to iterative denoising via flow matching. The key insight is to learn the flow (velocity) from noise to data and integrate it over time to generate samples.

The learning objective is:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim p_0(x_0), x_1 \sim p_1(x_1), t \sim U[0, 1]} \|(x_1-x_0) - u_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2 \tag{B.5}$$

where $u_\theta(x_t, t)$ is our time-conditioned UNet that predicts the flow at position $x_t$ and time $t$.

2.2 Training the Time-Conditioned UNet

I added time conditioning to the UNet using FCBlocks that embed the scalar timestep $t$ and modulate the network's intermediate representations. The training setup was:

  • Dataset: MNIST training set
  • Batch size: 64
  • Initial learning rate: 1e-2
  • Hidden dimension: D = 64
  • Epochs: 10
  • Scheduler: Exponential LR decay with $\gamma = 0.1^{(1/\text{num\_epochs})}$
  • Number of timesteps: 50

2.3 Sampling from the Time-Conditioned UNet

Using the trained time-conditioned UNet, I sampled images by starting from pure noise $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and iteratively applying the predicted flow over 50 timesteps:

$$x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + \Delta t \cdot u_\theta(x_t, t)$$

2.5 Training the Class-Conditioned UNet

To gain control over which digit is generated, I added class conditioning to the UNet. The class label is encoded as a one-hot vector and embedded using FCBlocks, similar to time conditioning. A key addition is classifier-free guidance (CFG) dropout: during training, 10% of the time the class conditioning is dropped (set to zero), allowing the model to learn both conditional and unconditional generation.

  • Dataset: MNIST training set
  • Batch size: 64
  • Initial learning rate: 1e-2
  • Hidden dimension: D = 64
  • Epochs: 10
  • Scheduler: Exponential LR decay with $\gamma = 0.1^{(1/\text{num\_epochs})}$
  • Number of timesteps: 300
  • Unconditional dropout: $p_{\text{uncond}} = 0.1$

2.6 Sampling from the Class-Conditioned UNet

For sampling, I used classifier-free guidance with $\gamma = 5.0$ to improve sample quality:

$$\text{flow} = \text{flow}_{\text{uncond}} + \gamma \cdot (\text{flow}_{\text{cond}} - \text{flow}_{\text{uncond}})$$

This amplifies the influence of the class conditioning, producing more distinctive digits.

Training Without Learning Rate Scheduler

As an additional experiment, I trained the class-conditioned UNet without the exponential learning rate scheduler. To compensate for the loss of the scheduler, I made the following adjustments:

Compensations for Removing the LR Scheduler

  1. Reduced initial learning rate from 1e-2 to 1e-3: The exponential scheduler decays the LR by a factor of 10 over training. Starting with a lower LR prevents the model from overshooting early in training.
  2. Increased epochs from 10 to 15: With a constant lower LR, we need more iterations to achieve similar convergence.
  3. Added gradient clipping (max_norm=1.0): This stabilizes training by preventing gradient explosions that the decaying LR would have naturally mitigated. :)

Part B Conclusion

This part of the project demonstrated the implementation of flow matching from scratch for generative modeling on MNIST. The key takeaways are:

Key Takeaways

  • Single-step denoising fails for generation: Training a model to denoise pure noise in one step produces blurry averages of all training images due to the MSE loss minimizing distance to the centroid of all possible outputs.
  • Iterative denoising via flow matching works: By learning the velocity field from noise to data and integrating over many timesteps, we can generate clear, diverse samples.
  • Time conditioning is necessary: The UNet must know "where" in the denoising process it is to make appropriate predictions at each step.
  • Class conditioning enables control: Adding class labels as conditioning allows us to generate specific digits on demand.
  • Classifier-free guidance improves quality: By training with random dropout of the class condition and using CFG at inference time, we get sharper, more distinctive samples.
  • Learning rate scheduling can be replaced: With careful tuning of initial LR, more epochs, and gradient clipping, we can achieve similar results without a scheduler.
